## 1. Why Dermatologists Choose Drugstore Products

Before revealing specific product recommendations, understanding why dermatologists prefer certain drugstore products over luxury alternatives provides crucial context for evaluating skincare intelligently rather than being swayed by marketing.

**Active Ingredients Are Active Ingredients Regardless of Price**

The fundamental truth underlying dermatologist drugstore preferences is that active ingredients—the molecules that actually produce skincare benefits—function identically regardless of the product price point or brand prestige containing them.

Hyaluronic acid hydrates skin by attracting and retaining moisture whether it comes in a $10 drugstore serum or a $200 luxury formula. The hyaluronic acid molecule doesn't work harder or better when suspended in expensive packaging—it performs the same biological function at the same efficacy when formulated at comparable concentrations and molecular weights.

Retinol stimulates cellular turnover and collagen production through the same biochemical pathways whether purchased from a drugstore or luxury boutique. A 0.5% retinol concentration produces equivalent results regardless of whether the tube costs $15 or $150, assuming proper formulation and stability.

Niacinamide improves skin barrier function, reduces inflammation, and regulates oil production identically in a $8 drugstore moisturizer or a $120 luxury serum when present at the same 5% concentration. The niacinamide molecules don't know or care about their product's price tag.

This biochemical reality means that comparing products should focus on active ingredient identity, concentration, and formulation quality rather than price, brand, or marketing claims. Dermatologists understand this, leading them to choose products based on functional performance rather than prestige factors.

**Formulation Quality Exists at All Price Points**

Effective skincare requires more than just active ingredients—proper formulation ensures those actives penetrate skin appropriately, remain stable until use, and work synergistically with other formula components. Some assume luxury brands have superior formulation expertise, but this isn't necessarily true.

Many major drugstore skincare brands are owned by the same parent companies producing luxury lines, using the same research facilities, similar formulation technologies, and sometimes even identical manufacturing plants. The difference lies in marketing budgets, packaging costs, and brand positioning rather than fundamental formulation capabilities.

CeraVe, a dermatologist-favorite drugstore brand, was literally developed with dermatologists to create effective, affordable formulations using ceramides and proper pH levels. La Roche-Posay and Vichy, positioned as "pharmacy" or "dermocosmetique" brands with moderate pricing, are owned by L'Oréal which also owns luxury brands like Lancôme and Giorgio Armani Beauty—they share research and formulation expertise across price points.

Pharmaceutical companies producing drugstore skincare often have superior research capabilities and quality control compared to boutique luxury brands because they apply pharmaceutical manufacturing standards to cosmetic products, ensuring consistency, stability, and purity that some luxury brands don't match.

**Dermatologists Focus on Evidence Over Marketing**

Medical training emphasizes evidence-based practice—making decisions based on scientific research, clinical trials, and documented outcomes rather than anecdote, tradition, or persuasive marketing. Dermatologists apply this same evidence-based approach to skincare product evaluation.

Luxury skincare marketing often emphasizes novel ingredients, proprietary complexes, exotic botanicals, and revolutionary technologies that sound impressive but lack substantial clinical evidence supporting their efficacy. Dermatologists remain skeptical of these claims until seeing peer-reviewed research demonstrating benefits.

In contrast, many affordable drugstore products contain well-researched ingredients with decades of clinical evidence supporting their effectiveness. Retinol, niacinamide, alpha hydroxy acids, benzoyl peroxide, ceramides, hyaluronic acid—these proven actives appear repeatedly in affordable drugstore products because they work, not because they're trendy or novel.

When dermatologists choose personal skincare, they select products with proven track records and solid evidence rather than exciting-sounding but unproven luxury formulations, leading them consistently toward affordable evidence-based options.

**Cost-Effectiveness Matters for Patient Recommendations**

Dermatologists don't just choose drugstore products for themselves—they frequently recommend them to patients because they understand that skincare effectiveness requires consistent long-term use, and expensive products that patients can't afford to repurchase won't be used consistently.

A $150 serum used once because patients can't afford replacement produces far worse outcomes than a $15 serum used consistently for months and years. Dermatologists recognize that cost-effectiveness directly impacts compliance and therefore results.

Additionally, many patients simply can't afford luxury skincare regardless of how effective it might be. Recommending affordable effective alternatives ensures all patients can access quality skincare regardless of socioeconomic status, aligning with medicine's ethical commitment to equitable care.

**Simplicity Beats Complexity**

Luxury skincare often involves elaborate multi-step routines with 8-12 different products used morning and night. This complexity seems sophisticated but often overwhelms patients leading to inconsistent use or complete abandonment of routines.

Dermatologists prefer simple routines with a few key products containing proven actives—cleanser, moisturizer, sunscreen, and maybe one or two targeted treatments. This simplicity enables consistent use and reduces the risk of product interactions, excessive irritation, or confusion about proper application.

Drugstore products enable building these simple effective routines inexpensively, while replicating the same routine with luxury products might cost 10-20 times more without improving results.

**Personal Experience Validates Drugstore Efficacy**

Many dermatologists personally used affordable drugstore products during medical school and residency when budgets were tight, discovering through direct experience that these products produce excellent results. This personal validation reinforces their professional understanding that price doesn't equal efficacy.

Having seen their own skin respond beautifully to affordable products, dermatologists feel confident recommending these same products to patients and continuing to use them despite now being able to afford luxury alternatives if desired.

**Industry Relationships Don't Bias Toward Luxury**

[While some dermatologists ](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/adult-health/in-depth/skin-care/art-20048237)have relationships with skincare companies through research, consulting, or speaking engagements, these relationships exist across both luxury and affordable brands. Many dermatologists deliberately avoid financial relationships with skincare companies to maintain prescribing and recommending independence.

When dermatologists without financial conflicts consistently choose certain drugstore products, it validates these products' quality without commercial bias influencing recommendations.

**Ingredient Concentration Transparency**

Drugstore brands, particularly those marketing to medically-informed consumers, often provide clear ingredient concentration information enabling proper evaluation. You can find 10% niacinamide serums, 2% salicylic acid cleansers, or 0.3% retinol creams with concentrations clearly stated.

Some luxury brands deliberately obscure active ingredient concentrations, listing ingredients without percentages and relying on marketing language rather than transparent formulation details. This lack of transparency frustrates dermatologists who want to know exactly what they're recommending or using.

**Availability and Accessibility**

Drugstore products are available everywhere—grocery stores, pharmacies, online retailers, mass merchants—making them easy to purchase, repurchase, and access even when traveling. Luxury products often require visiting specific retailers, ordering online with shipping delays, or dealing with limited availability.

This universal accessibility means dermatologists can recommend products patients can actually find and buy easily rather than requiring special trips or online ordering that might never happen.

**Less Problematic Ingredients**

Ironically, many affordable drugstore products contain fewer potentially problematic ingredients than luxury alternatives. Luxury skincare often includes fragrance, essential oils, exotic botanical extracts, and numerous additional ingredients creating sensory experiences and justifying high prices but potentially irritating sensitive skin.

Budget-focused drugstore brands often use simpler formulations with fewer ingredients, reducing irritation risk and making products suitable for sensitive skin. Dermatologists appreciate this straightforward approach focusing on effective actives rather than sensory luxury.

**Cleanser Principles Dermatologists Follow**

Beyond specific product recommendations, dermatologists apply consistent principles when selecting cleansers:

Gentle over harsh—cleansers don't need to foam heavily, tingle, or leave skin "squeaky clean" to work effectively. These sensations often indicate stripping and damage rather than thoroughness.

pH-balanced formulations respecting skin's natural acid mantle (pH 4.5-5.5) work better than alkaline soaps disrupting skin's protective barrier.

Minimal unnecessary ingredients reduce irritation risk—fragrance, dyes, harsh preservatives, and exotic botanicals don't improve cleansing but potentially cause problems.

Proper cleansing technique matters as much as product choice—gentle massage for 30-60 seconds, lukewarm water, soft pat drying, and immediate moisturizer application maximize cleanser benefits regardless of specific product used.

Double cleansing (oil-based cleanser followed by water-based cleanser) isn't necessary for most people—a single good cleanser suffices unless wearing heavy makeup or sunscreen requiring extra removal help.

Morning cleansing can be gentler or even just water for some people—skin doesn't accumulate significant dirt overnight, so aggressive morning cleansing often does more harm than good.

**What Dermatologists Avoid in Cleansers**

Understanding what dermatologists avoid helps evaluate cleanser quality:

Harsh sulfates like sodium lauryl sulfate create satisfying foam but strip skin excessively. Gentler surfactants work better.

Added fragrance serves no functional purpose and commonly causes irritation and allergic reactions.

Antibacterial ingredients like triclosan aren't necessary for facial cleansing and potentially disrupt healthy skin microbiome.

Scrubbing beads or physical exfoliants in daily cleansers often irritate more than help—chemical exfoliants used separately work better.

"Natural" or "organic" marketing doesn't guarantee gentleness—many natural ingredients irritate more than synthetic alternatives.

Cleansing selection represents the crucial first step in dermatologist-approved routines, and the good news is that excellent options cost $10-15 and last months. CeraVe, Cetaphil, La Roche-Posay, Neutrogena, and Vanicream all offer dermatologist-trusted cleansers delivering gentle effective cleansing without stripping or irritation. Choose based on your skin type and texture preference, knowing any of these options provides professional-quality cleansing at drugstore prices.

**Moisturizer Principles Dermatologists Follow**

Key principles guide dermatologist moisturizer selection and use:

Layer moisturizer on damp skin immediately after cleansing to seal in moisture rather than waiting until skin dries completely.

More expensive doesn't mean more effective—luxury moisturizers often cost more due to packaging, fragrance, and exotic ingredients that don't improve hydration.

Simple formulations with proven humectants (hyaluronic acid, glycerin), emollients (ceramides, fatty acids), and occlusives (petrolatum, dimethicone) outperform complex formulations with trendy but unproven ingredients.

Different seasons or environments might require adjusting moisturizer—lighter in summer or humid climates, richer in winter or dry climates.

Body skin often needs different, richer moisturizers than facial skin, and using affordable face moisturizers on body becomes expensive—large pump bottles of body lotions with similar ingredients cost less per ounce.

Moisturizer should feel comfortable, not greasy, tight, or irritating—if your moisturizer causes problems, try a different formulation rather than skipping moisturizer entirely.

**What Dermatologists Avoid in Moisturizers**

Avoid these common moisturizer pitfalls:

Jar packaging exposing product to air and fingers repeatedly reduces stability and introduces contamination—pump or tube packaging works better.

Added fragrance provides no skincare benefit while being a leading cause of irritation and allergic reactions.

Long exotic ingredient lists with numerous botanical extracts increase irritation risk without proven benefits.

## 9. What Dermatologists Avoid in Drugstore Aisles

Understanding what dermatologists skip helps navigate drugstore skincare aisles more effectively, avoiding products that don't deliver value or potentially cause problems.

**Added Fragrance**

Dermatologists overwhelmingly avoid products with added fragrance whether synthetic or "natural" essential oils.

Fragrance provides no skincare benefit while being the leading cause of cosmetic allergic contact dermatitis and irritation.

"Fragrance" on ingredient lists can represent hundreds of different chemicals with no disclosure requirements, making it impossible to identify specific allergens.

Natural fragrances from essential oils often irritate more than synthetic fragrances despite "natural" marketing appeal.

Fragrance-free differs from unscented—unscented might contain masking fragrances hiding other odors, while fragrance-free contains no fragrance ingredients.

Even products smelling pleasant to you might trigger skin reactions or allergies, making fragrance-free safer default choice.

**Jar Packaging**

Jars exposing products to air and fingers repeatedly reduce stability and introduce contamination.

Active ingredients like vitamin C and retinol degrade when exposed to air and light, making jar packaging particularly problematic for these actives.

Fingers dipping into jars introduce bacteria potentially contaminating products.

Pump or tube packaging protects products better while also controlling dosing and reducing waste.

Some luxury brands deliberately use jar packaging despite its drawbacks because jars look luxurious and feel high-end even though they compromise product quality.

**Apricot Scrubs and Physical Exfoliants**

Harsh physical exfoliants with irregular particles cause micro-tears in skin rather than providing beneficial exfoliation.

St. Ives Apricot Scrub became infamous among dermatologists as example of damaging physical exfoliant despite its popularity.

Chemical exfoliants (AHAs, BHAs) provide superior exfoliation without mechanical damage to skin.

If using physical exfoliants, choose products with smooth round beads rather than irregular crushed particles, and use gentle pressure rather than aggressive scrubbing.

Most people don't need daily mechanical exfoliation—chemical exfoliants 2-3x weekly work better.

**"Natural" and "Organic" Marketing**

Natural ingredients aren't inherently safer, gentler, or more effective than synthetic alternatives—sometimes they're worse.

Poison ivy is natural but certainly not skin-friendly. Arsenic is natural but toxic. "Natural" doesn't mean safe or beneficial.

Synthetic ingredients often have better safety profiles, more consistent quality, less contamination risk, and more extensive testing than botanical alternatives.

Essential oils marketed as "natural" fragrances often irritate more than synthetic fragrances.

Focus on ingredient efficacy and safety evidence rather than natural vs. synthetic as selection criterion.

**Multi-Level Marketing Skincare**

MLM skincare brands sold through independent distributors rather than retail stores generally disappoint dermatologists despite aggressive marketing and high prices.

Products often cost significantly more than drugstore alternatives with similar ingredients due to MLM distribution structure requiring multiple commission levels.

Scientific evidence supporting efficacy claims often lacking or less rigorous than established brands.

Pushy sales tactics and income opportunity pitches confuse skincare purchasing with business opportunities.

Dermatologists generally recommend avoiding MLM skincare in favor of evidence-based drugstore or prescription alternatives.

**Toners and Astringents (Usually)**

Most toners serve no essential function in modern skincare routines, making them optional steps dermatologists often skip.

Historical toners removed soap residue when harsh soaps were primary cleansers, but modern gentle cleansers don't leave residue requiring toner removal.

Alcohol-based astringents dry and irritate skin without providing lasting benefits.

If using toners, choose hydrating or pH-adjusting versions rather than harsh alcohol-based astringents.

Chemical exfoliant toners (like glycolic acid toners) serve useful purpose, but they're treatments rather than traditional toners.

Many dermatologists simply skip toners entirely, moving from cleanser directly to treatments and moisturizer.

**Makeup Wipes**

Convenient but problematic, makeup wipes often don't cleanse thoroughly while causing irritation through friction and harsh surfactants.

Leave residue and don't fully remove makeup, sunscreen, or impurities requiring actual cleansing.

Rough wiping causes irritation and premature aging through mechanical stress on delicate facial skin.

Wasteful single-use products with environmental impact.

Dermatologists prefer proper cleansing with gentle cleansers and water over makeup wipes whenever possible.

If using wipes for convenience while traveling or at gym, follow with proper cleansing when possible.

**DIY Skincare Ingredients**

Lemon juice, baking soda, apple cider vinegar, and other kitchen ingredients often suggested in DIY skincare advice typically cause more harm than good.

Lemon juice is far too acidic and photosensitizing potentially causing chemical burns and sun sensitivity.

Baking soda is too alkaline, disrupting skin's protective acid mantle and causing irritation.

Apple cider vinegar in undiluted form causes chemical burns.

Sugar and salt scrubs cause mechanical damage similar to apricot scrubs.

Stick to properly formulated skincare products with appropriate pH and concentrations rather than kitchen experiments.

**Expensive "Miracle" Creams at Drugstores**

Some drugstore brands offer premium lines with luxury pricing that dermatologists view skeptically.

These products usually don't offer proportional improvements justifying 3-5x higher prices compared to brand's regular lines.

Marketing emphasizes exotic ingredients or proprietary complexes without substantial evidence supporting superior efficacy.

If spending $40-80 on drugstore "prestige" products, consider whether prescription alternatives or established pharmacy brands (La Roche-Posay, Vichy) provide better value.

**Products Making Dramatic Claims**

"Erases wrinkles overnight," "miracle cream," "better than Botox," and similar dramatic claims signal marketing over science.

If claims sound too good to be true, they are. Dramatic skin improvements require time, consistency, and often professional treatments.

Evidence-based ingredients work but require realistic expectations and patience.

Dermatologists trust products making modest evidence-based claims over those promising miracles.

**What Dermatologists Also Question**

Even some popular product categories face dermatologist skepticism:

Sheet masks provide temporary hydration but don't offer unique benefits beyond good moisturizer applied to damp skin.

Facial oils often provide less complete moisturizing than well-formulated moisturizers with humectants, emollients, and occlusives working synergistically.

Pore strips remove superficial debris but don't address underlying causes and can irritate skin.

Clay masks provide temporary tightening sensation without meaningful long-term benefits for most people.

These products aren't necessarily harmful but often don't deliver value proportional to their cost and hype.

Understanding what dermatologists avoid helps navigate drugstore aisles more effectively, skipping products that won't deliver benefits or might cause problems. Focus on fragrance-free products in pump or tube packaging with evidence-based ingredients and realistic claims rather than exotic natural ingredients, harsh physical exfoliants, or miracle promises. Dermatologists' avoidance patterns reflect evidence-based thinking prioritizing safety, efficacy, and value over marketing hype.

**Routine Building Principles**

Dermatologists follow consistent principles when building routines:

**Simplicity over complexity** - 3-6 products total works better than 10-15 step routines. More products increase cost, time commitment, and complication risk without proportional benefits.

**Introduce products slowly** - Add one new product at a time, waiting 1-2 weeks before adding another to identify any reactions and assess individual product effects.

**Start gentle, increase gradually** - Begin actives at low concentrations and infrequent application (2-3x weekly), building to higher concentrations or daily use as tolerance develops.

**Layer correctly** - Cleanser → treatments (thinnest to thickest) → moisturizer → sunscreen. Apply products to damp skin when possible for better absorption.

**Consistency beats perfection** - Simple routine used daily produces better results than perfect routine used sporadically. Choose products and routines you'll actually use consistently.

**Adjust for seasons** - Lighter products in summer, richer in winter. More hydration in dry climates, oil control in humid climates.

**Listen to your skin** - Irritation, excessive dryness, or reactions require adjusting routine. Skincare should feel comfortable, not painful or uncomfortable.

**Patience is essential** - Most products require 8-12 weeks of consistent use to show significant results. Evaluate effectiveness over months, not days.

**When to Simplify**

Reduce routine complexity if:

- Experiencing irritation, redness, or increased sensitivity
- Spending more than 10-15 minutes twice daily on skincare
- Unable to maintain consistency due to routine complexity
- Products conflicting or causing pilling/separation
- Budget strain from maintaining numerous products

Strip back to basics (cleanser, moisturizer, sunscreen) and slowly reintroduce products one at a time.

**When to Seek Professional Help**

Consult dermatologist when:

- Acne not improving after 12 weeks of consistent over-the-counter treatment
- Severe cystic acne or acne causing scarring
- Suspicious moles or skin changes
- Rashes, eczema, or dermatitis not responding to gentle care
- Excessive hair loss or nail changes
- Desire for professional treatments (lasers, prescription retinoids, etc.)

acid, ceramides, retinol, AHAs, benzoyl peroxide at meaningful concentrations typically 2-10% depending on ingredient. (2) Formulation quality indicated by appropriate pH for actives, stable packaging protecting ingredients from degradation, and absence of unnecessary irritants like fragrance. (3) Brand reputation among dermatologists—CeraVe, Cetaphil, La Roche-Posay, Neutrogena, Vanicream, The Ordinary, and others mentioned in this guide have earned professional trust through consistent quality. **Q: Should I use different products for different seasons or stick with the same routine year-round?** Seasonal adjustment makes sense for most people because environmental conditions affect skin differently across seasons. Winter's dry air and indoor heating often require richer moisturizers, more intensive hydration, and potentially reduced active use if skin becomes sensitive. Summer's heat and humidity might enable lighter moisturizers, more frequent cleansing if sweating, and higher SPF or more frequent sunscreen reapplication during outdoor activities. However, core routine elements—gentle cleansing, sun protection, key actives addressing your concerns—remain consistent while specific product weights and formulations adjust. Listen to your skin rather than following rigid seasonal schedules—some people need minimal adjustment while others benefit from significant seasonal product changes.

**Q: Can I use the same products dermatologists recommend even if I have sensitive skin, rosacea, or eczema?** Many dermatologist-recommended products specifically suit sensitive skin—Vanicream, Cetaphil, fragrance-free CeraVe products, and La Roche-Posay Toleriane line are developed for sensitive or compromised skin. For diagnosed conditions like rosacea or eczema, work with your dermatologist to identify specific triggers and appropriate products—while many drugstore options work well for these conditions, professional guidance helps navigate individual sensitivities. General principles remain: avoid fragrance and harsh ingredients, choose simple formulations, introduce products slowly, and be very gentle with actives or skip them entirely if skin is too reactive.

**Q: How long should I try a new product before deciding if it works?** For basic moisturizers and cleansers, you'll know within 1-2 weeks if they suit your skin or cause irritation. For active treatments (retinoids, AHAs, vitamin C, niacinamide), give 8-12 weeks of consistent use before evaluating effectiveness because these actives work gradually by changing cellular processes and skin structure over time. If products cause obvious irritation, burning, or allergic reactions within days, discontinue immediately. However, mild adjustment period with slight dryness or sensitivity when starting actives is normal—persevere through initial adjustment unless reactions are severe. The key is distinguishing normal adjustment from actual problems requiring discontinuation.

**Q: Can I mix drugstore and luxury products in my routine or should I stick to one or the other?** Mix freely based on what works for your skin and budget—skincare isn't about brand loyalty. Many people use primarily drugstore basics (cleanser, moisturizer, sunscreen) while splurging on one luxury serum they love for psychological enjoyment rather than necessity. Others use entirely drugstore routines and achieve excellent results. The products don't know or care what brands they're layered with—they interact based on ingredients and formulation rather than price points. Use this freedom to optimize your routine: afford more products by choosing drugstore for basics, splurge on luxury for products you truly love if it brings you joy, but understand that functional effectiveness comes from ingredients not brands.

**Q: Why don't more people know about these affordable alternatives if they work so well?** Information asymmetry and marketing power keep consumers unaware. Luxury brands spend millions on advertising, influencer partnerships, magazine placements, and sophisticated marketing creating aspirational narratives around expensive products. Drugstore brands have smaller marketing budgets and less aspirational positioning. Beauty media depends on luxury brand advertising revenue, creating conflicts of interest against promoting cheap alternatives. Influencers earn more from luxury brand sponsorships than drugstore collaborations. Dermatologists sharing these truths don't have marketing budgets competing with beauty industry spending. Additionally, many people assume price indicates quality ("you get what you pay for") and don't research evidence about ingredient effectiveness. As more dermatologists share this information publicly through social media, books, and articles, awareness grows, but overcoming massive marketing spending requires grassroots education.

**Q: Do dermatologists actually personally use these drugstore products or do they just recommend them to patients while using luxury products themselves?** Many dermatologists genuinely use these drugstore products personally—documented through social media where dermatologists show their actual routines, interviews where they discuss personal skincare, and surveys of dermatology communities revealing product preferences. Dermatologists who could easily afford any luxury products often choose affordable alternatives because they understand that ingredients and formulations matter more than branding. Some dermatologists do use a mix of drugstore and prescription products (which they can access through their practices) or pharmaceutical/medical-grade products from companies like SkinCeuticals or EltaMD occupying middle ground between pure drugstore and ultra-luxury. However, the core truth remains: dermatologists consistently choose evidence-based products regardless of price, and many of those evidence-based products cost $10-20 at drugstores.

**Q: Should I follow a Korean 10-step skincare routine or stick with the simple dermatologist-recommended routines in this guide?** Dermatologists generally favor simplicity over elaborate multi-step routines. Korean skincare philosophy emphasizes hydration, gentle care, and preventive approach—all dermatologist-approved principles. However, 10-step routines risk product redundancy (multiple hydrating steps that don't provide additive benefits), excessive cost and time investment reducing sustainability, increased irritation risk from numerous products and potential incompatibilities, and complexity making it difficult to identify what actually helps if you have reactions. Core Korean skincare principles—double cleansing for heavy makeup/sunscreen, generous hydration, sun protection, gentle care—are excellent. But you can achieve these through simplified 4-6 step routines incorporating key principles without literal 10-step implementation. Use whatever routine you'll actually maintain consistently—if elaborate rituals bring you joy and you sustain them, great. If simplicity enables consistency, choose simple evidence-based approaches.

**Q: What's the one drugstore product dermatologists recommend most universally across all skin types and concerns?** Sunscreen. If dermatologists could mandate one product universally regardless of age, skin type, or concerns, it would be broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen used daily. Sun protection prevents skin cancer, premature aging, hyperpigmentation, and numerous other skin problems more effectively than any other single intervention. Affordable effective options like Neutrogena Ultra Sheer ($10), CeraVe Hydrating Mineral ($15), or La Roche-Posay Anthelios ($36 for large size providing excellent value) make consistent daily sun protection accessible to everyone. While other product needs vary by skin type and concerns, everyone benefits from daily sun protection regardless of other skincare choices.
